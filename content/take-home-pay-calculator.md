## What's Deducted From Your Gross Salary

Your gross salary goes through several deductions before it becomes your take-home pay. Here's each component in the order it's calculated:

### 1. Income Tax

The largest deduction. Based on Australia's [progressive tax brackets](/tax-brackets/), your tax is calculated in stages — not at a single flat rate. On $80,000, you pay $14,788 in income tax (an effective rate of 18.49%).

### 2. Medicare Levy

A flat 2% of your taxable income that funds Medicare. On $80,000, that's $1,600 per year ($30.77 per week). Low-income earners under approximately $27,222 may pay a reduced rate or be exempt. [Full Medicare levy guide →](/medicare-levy/)

### 3. HECS-HELP Repayment (If Applicable)

If you have a student loan and your income exceeds $67,000, compulsory repayments apply. The new FY2025-26 marginal system charges 15 cents per dollar above the threshold — not a flat rate on your total income like the old system. [Calculate your exact HECS repayment →](/hecs-help-calculator/)

### 4. Superannuation (Does NOT Reduce Take-Home)

Your employer pays 12% of your Ordinary Time Earnings into your super fund. On $80,000, that's $9,600 per year. This is paid on top of your salary — it appears on your breakdown for reference but doesn't reduce the amount you take home. [Super calculator →](/superannuation-calculator/)

## Gross vs Net Pay — What's the Difference?

**Gross pay** is your total salary before any deductions — the number in your employment contract.

**Net pay** (or take-home pay) is what you actually receive after income tax, Medicare levy, and any HECS repayments are deducted.

For example:

| Component | Amount (on $80,000) |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Gross Annual Salary | $80,000 |
| Income Tax | −$14,788 |
| Medicare Levy (2%) | −$1,600 |
| HECS Repayment | $0 (if no debt) |
| **Net Take-Home Pay** | **$63,612** |
| Per Week | $1,223.31 |
| Per Fortnight | $2,446.62 |
| Per Month | $5,301.00 |

Your employer also pays $9,600 in super on top of your salary, making your total package $89,600.

## How to Increase Your Take-Home Pay

### Salary Sacrifice Into Super

By directing part of your pre-tax salary into super, you reduce your taxable income — which means less income tax. On $100,000, sacrificing $10,000 into super could save you approximately $1,500 in tax, though the money goes to your super rather than your bank account.

→ [Compare your pay before and after salary sacrifice](/salary-sacrifice-calculator/)

### Claim Work-Related Deductions

Tax deductions reduce your taxable income. Common deductions include work-related travel, uniforms, tools, professional memberships, and home office expenses. These reduce your tax at your [marginal rate](/tax-brackets/) — a $1,000 deduction at the 30% bracket saves you $300 in tax.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How is take-home pay calculated?
Take-home pay is your gross salary minus income tax, Medicare levy, and any HECS repayments. Your employer deducts these through the PAYG system before you receive your pay. Super is paid separately by your employer and doesn't reduce your take-home.

### What percentage of my salary do I take home?
It depends on your salary. At $50,000, you take home about 86.4% ($43,212). At $100,000, it's about 77.2% ($77,212 without HECS). The percentage decreases as your salary increases because higher income is taxed at higher marginal rates.

### Is super deducted from my pay?
No. Your employer pays super (12% of your Ordinary Time Earnings) on top of your salary. It doesn't come out of your take-home pay. However, if you choose to [salary sacrifice](/salary-sacrifice-calculator/) additional amounts into super, those are deducted pre-tax.

### Why is my first pay smaller than expected?
If you haven't submitted a Tax File Number (TFN) declaration, your employer is required to withhold tax at the highest marginal rate (45% plus Medicare). Submit your TFN declaration immediately to be taxed at the correct rate.
